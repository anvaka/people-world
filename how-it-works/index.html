People World, step by step

People World draws an imaginary map of the world’s countries. Every country is there, with its real neighbours and its real population, on land that never existed. This page follows one map, world 1, from a list of numbers to the finished drawing, one step at a time.

Every picture below is world 1 at that step, drawn from the generator’s own data. Each step ends with a folded list of the functions that do the work and links to their code, and all 138 algorithms are listed at the end.

The puzzle

The input is one file. For each of 250 countries and territories it gives a name, a continent and a population, and it lists 322 pairs of countries that share a land border. France, for example, has 68.7 million people and borders Andorra, Belgium, Germany, Italy, Luxembourg, Monaco, Spain and Switzerland. Nothing in the file says where anything is.

From that list the map has to keep three rules.

  1. Two countries that share a border in the list share a border on the map.
  2. Two countries that don’t share a border in the list never touch on the map.
  3. Each country covers one square unit of map for every million people. India gets 1,464 units, and Pitcairn, with 46 people, gets 0.000046 of one.

The rules pull against each other. Russia has fewer people than Bangladesh (144 million against 176 million), so on this map it has less land than Bangladesh. Yet it has to touch fourteen countries, from China to Estonia, and touch nobody else.

The generator solves the puzzle in a few seconds, in two halves. First it decides who sits where and which borders exist, working on a mosaic of small cells. Then it turns the cells into smooth lines and fits every area exactly. After that it adds mountains, rivers and climate, and a style draws the result.

1 Discs

Before any country has a shape, it gets a place. Each country becomes a disc whose area is its population, so the discs already have the right sizes. The goal of this step is an arrangement where every disc touches its neighbours’ discs and keeps clear of everyone else’s.

First, how far apart should two countries be? Neighbours should sit about one radius plus the other apart. For two countries that aren’t neighbours, follow the shortest chain of borders from one to the other and add up the gaps along the way. Spain and China are many borders apart, so they should sit far apart.

Now imagine every pair of countries tied together by a spring, each as long as that distance. Let go, and the springs push and pull until the total strain is as small as it can get. This method is called stress majorization, and it puts every region roughly where it belongs.

Springs get the big picture right and the details wrong. Some neighbours end up apart and some strangers overlap, so a second pass works on one pair at a time. Two neighbours are nudged until their discs overlap a little, and two strangers are pushed apart until there is a gap between them. Fixing one pair can spoil another, so the pass sweeps over all the pairs hundreds of times, until the corrections die away. Along the way it looks for two lines between neighbours that cross. On a flat map two crossing lines can’t both be borders, so one country is pushed back across the other line.

Countries with no land neighbours, like Japan and Madagascar, get no disc. They wait for step 8.

2 The borders that can’t all exist

Three houses each need a pipe to each of three wells, and no two pipes may cross. On paper, eight pipes go in easily and the ninth always crosses one, and it can be proved that no flat drawing does it.

That old puzzle is hidden in the real list of borders. Armenia, Turkey and Russia each border Georgia, Azerbaijan and Iran. Russia doesn’t touch Iran directly, but it reaches Iran through China and Pakistan, which works like one long pipe. Now put a dot in every country and draw a road from each dot to each neighbour’s dot, crossing their shared border. On any flat map these roads never cross. So no flat map can give all nine of these pairs a border.

The real Earth gets around it with an exclave. Azerbaijan’s border with Turkey belongs to Nakhchivan, a piece of Azerbaijan cut off from the rest of it by Armenia. A country in two pieces can meet neighbours that one piece couldn’t.

People World does the same on purpose. It looks for the fewest borders it has to give up so that the rest can be drawn flat, and turns each one into an exclave, a small piece of one country set into the other. For the world’s countries one is enough, and in world 1 it is Armenia–Georgia.

How does it know that one is the fewest? A test can tell in a single pass over the list whether it can be drawn flat (the left-right planarity test), and when it can’t, the code can pull out a knot like the one above. Every knot must lose at least one of its links. So the search keeps the knots found so far, picks the cheapest set of links that cuts every one of them, and tests what is left. If the rest is flat, nothing cheaper can exist, because anything cheaper would leave one of the known knots whole. If it isn’t flat, the test hands over a new knot and the search goes again. Among the sets with the fewest links, it prefers links that touch a small country, and links the layout of step 1 found hard to draw.

With that link set aside, the discs are laid out once more, without it. Then the land with the most countries, Eurasia and Africa together, is turned to lie along its long axis, and the second, the Americas, stands upright beside it.

3 Cells

Discs can’t be a map. They overlap in some places and leave gaps in others. Countries need shapes that fit together, and shapes are easiest to work out on a canvas of small pieces, like a mosaic. World 1 has 52,908 of them, called cells.

A country’s shape can only be as fine as its cells. A country of 80,000 people needs tiny cells to have any shape at all, while India would need millions of cells that size. So the cells are graded, small where small countries sit and larger the farther they are from one. The smallest cells are a sixteenth the width of the largest.

To make them, start from big square tiles and split any tile into four while it is larger than the size wanted there. Put one point in each tile, nudged off centre so the points don’t line up in rows. Join the points into triangles. Then cut each triangle into three equal parts, from its centre to the middle of each side. A point’s cell is its parts of all the triangles around it.

Cells made this way always fit together with no gaps and no overlaps, and two cells that touch always share a whole edge, never a lone corner. That gives touching an exact meaning. Two countries touch when a cell of one shares an edge with a cell of the other.

4 Paint

Now every cell gets an owner, either a country or the sea. Picture paint poured at the middle of every disc at once. Each country’s paint spreads outward cell by cell, and stops when it covers the country’s area.

A big country’s paint spreads faster, in proportion to its disc’s radius, so every country reaches the edge of its own disc at about the same time. A small country fills its disc and stops, and a big neighbour keeps flowing around it.

Paint never enters a cell that touches a country it may not touch, so the second rule holds from the very first cell. The cost of crossing a cell also rises and falls with a noise pattern, so the fronts advance unevenly and the borders come out irregular, as real ones are. Cells nobody claims stay sea.

Under the paint is a shortest-path search (Dijkstra’s) run from every country at once, with a queue that always hands over the cell the nearest paint would reach next, anywhere on the map.

5 Shaking

After growth the countries have the right sizes but ragged shapes, and some neighbours don’t touch yet. This step improves the map one cell at a time.

It needs a score for the whole map, where lower is better. Every bit of border adds to the score, coasts double, so compact countries score better. Being the wrong size adds to it. A cell far from the middle of its country adds to it, which pulls long arms back in. And a border between neighbours that is shorter than it should be adds a penalty.

Each trial takes a cell on the edge of a country and offers it to the country next door. If the score goes down, the move is made. If the score goes up, the move is sometimes made anyway, with a chance that is smaller the worse the move is. That chance shrinks as the run goes on. Early on, the map can get worse for a while to escape a bad arrangement, and by the end almost only improvements pass. This is simulated annealing, named after the way a metal cooled slowly settles into a strong structure. World 1 gets 30 sweeps over every cell on an edge.

Some moves are never made, whatever they do to the score. A country must stay in one piece. It may not touch a country it isn’t linked to. A strait between strangers stays at least two cells wide. And a border two countries need doesn’t shrink below a minimum length. Checking all of that across the map after each of hundreds of thousands of moves would take far too long, so each check looks only at the ring of cells around the moving cell. If the country losing the cell appears in that ring as one unbroken run, taking the cell can’t split the country in two.

6 Repairs

The score can’t fix everything. In world 1, after the first shaking, twelve pairs of neighbours still don’t touch, and a run of repairs follows.

First come bridges. Each country of a missing pair grows an arm toward the other, along the cheapest path through sea, or through a third country when it has to, and a few sweeps of shaking then fatten the arm. In world 1 this restores ten of the twelve borders.

A small country that still can’t reach a neighbour is lifted off the map and grown again where its neighbours are closest together. This repair is a trial. The map is saved first, and if the move doesn’t help, it is undone.

Bridges can leave snakes, so any part of a country too far from its middle is cut off and given to a neighbour or to the sea. A comment in the code says it plainly: a few more exclaves are better than snakes.

Every border still missing then becomes an exclave, and so does the one given up in step 2. Usually the exclave is a piece of the smaller country, about an eighth of it or less, carved into the larger one. When one country lacks borders with two countries that touch each other, a single piece on their shared border serves both, and that is the piece of Georgia from step 2.

Last come the seas. Coasts cost score, so shaking squeezes the water between strangers into hairline canals. Long canals are widened into seas with ragged shores, and the new sea is frozen so that later shaking can’t close it again.

7 From cells to lines

So far every border is a staircase of cell edges. The second half of the generator turns the staircases into lines and fits every area exactly.

Each stretch of border, from one place where three countries meet to the next, becomes a single chain of points, shared by the countries on both sides. Because both sides use the same chain, a border can never open a gap or overlap as it moves. From here on the map changes only by moving points, and every move is checked first. No line may cross another, and no line may come too close to one it must stay apart from. A move that fails is refused, or tried again shorter. That is how the borders settled on the cells survive everything that follows.

Smoothing comes first. Each point moves part of the way toward the middle of its two neighbours. Done plainly, that shrinks every shape a little each time, the small ones most, so the steps alternate, one pulling the line in and the next pushing it out a little more (Taubin smoothing). The zigzags go, and the sizes stay.

Then the lines are roughened, because real coasts have capes and coves at every scale. Each point moves sideways by fractal noise, which is bumps of one size plus bumps half as tall and twice as fine, and so on. Coasts get the full amount and borders about a third, a gentle wobble.

The cells can’t draw a country smaller than about twelve of the smallest cells, so the tiniest countries were drawn too big. Each now shrinks toward a point inside it, down to its true size, and its neighbours’ borders stretch to follow it, so it keeps every neighbour. Wherever two countries that must not touch come too close, both sides are pushed apart, so a reader sees water between them.

The last pass fits the areas. If a country’s outline is P long and moves outward by w, the country grows by about P × w, so a country short of its area by d pushes its outline out by d ÷ P. A border moves by the average of what its two countries want. A coast moves for its one country, and the sea takes up the difference. A rough fit already ran before the roughening, and this one repeats until every country is within a tenth of a percent of its size. In world 1 the worst is 0.05% off.

The project ships with a checker that builds worlds and tests the rules. For world 1 it reports all 322 borders present, no false ones, and every country within 1% of its size.

8 Islands

83 countries have no land neighbours in the list, among them Japan, Madagascar, Australia and most of the Caribbean and the Pacific. Their rules are easy, so they come last. Each is a blob, a circle with every point pushed in or out by smooth noise and then scaled until its area comes out exact. Its place is the nearest open sea, searched ring by ring outward from the middle of its continent’s countries, largest island first. That keeps the Caribbean near the Americas and Japan near Asia.

A few pieces of land hold two countries that border only each other, like Britain and Ireland. They are laid out as small lands of their own in step 1, then put in the open water nearest the middle of their continent.

9 Mountains and rivers

The countries are done. Now the land gets a shape of its own. This part is an add-on, which can be switched off, and every style draws the same mountains and rivers from it.

Height rises with distance from the sea, fast near the coast and then levelling off, with noise at three scales so the land has basins and bends. Every border stands on a low ridge, which keeps rivers from running along borders, where a reader could mistake one for the other.

For the water, picture the sea rising slowly over the land. It always comes in at the lowest point on its edge, so it fills each hollow to the rim before it spills on. Run in reverse, this tells every patch of ground which way its water drains, and no water stays trapped in a pit. This is called a priority flood. Going through the patches from the highest down, each adds its own rain to what it received and passes the total on, so every patch knows how much rain falls on all the land above it. Where enough water gathers, there is a river.

Mountains go where waters part. Between two large river basins runs a divide, and the highest stretches of divides become mountain ranges. About three in ten of the longer borders get a range of their own, the way the Pyrenees run between France and Spain. Then the rain runs off once more, now over the mountains and with more rain on the heights, and the final rivers are traced, largest first. A smaller river ends on the larger one it joins, and no two cross. World 1 has 113 rivers. Each also gets the valley it would have cut, wider and deeper the more water it carries.

10 Weather and what grows

Climate is another add-on. In every world the south is warm and the north cold, and high ground is colder. Wind blows in three belts, as on the Earth, with trade winds in the south, westerlies in the middle and easterlies in the far north. In half the worlds they blow the other way round.

To find how wet a place is, the code asks where its air came from. From each patch of land it walks back against the wind until it reaches the sea or has gone far enough, then replays the trip forward. Air off the sea starts full of moisture and loses some over every stretch of land, more where the ground climbs. So the coast the wind reaches first is the wettest, the heart of a continent is dry, and a mountain range takes the rain on its windward side and leaves a dry rain shadow behind it. Where the trade winds meet the westerlies the air is still and dry, and that is where the deserts lie. A river waters its banks whatever the sky does.

Warmth and moisture together decide what grows, from a table like the one the ecologist Robert Whittaker drew, five rows of warmth by four columns of moisture. Marsh goes on low wet ground near the sea, and the ground under the mountains is bare.

11 Drawing the map

Everything so far is data: outlines, borders, rivers, mountains and climate. A style turns it into a picture. People World has three styles. Ink looks like pen and ink on paper, realm is a painted relief drawn with WebGL, and atlas is a hand-coloured engraving, drawn by the ink style’s code with another look. Switching styles doesn’t build the world again.

The page itself places the names. It finds the point inside each country farthest from its border and puts the name there, and the ink style sets its lettering as large as the country allows, tilting the name of a long diagonal country along its length.

At the whole-world view a coast needs a handful of points, and zoomed in it needs thousands. Each line is thinned so that no point is off by more than 0.4 of a pixel at the current zoom, and the thinning is worked out once per line, so a new zoom only filters.

The ink style draws its hatching and seas in tiles, in background workers, and keeps them. While you pan or pinch, the page moves the picture it already has as one image, and draws properly once you stop. The same style puts its compass rose, ship and sea serpent in the widest stretches of open water. It already knows how far every patch of sea is from land, so the roomiest patch gets the compass rose, the next one far enough away gets the ship, then the serpent, then the names of the seas. All three are in the picture at the top.

Realm lights its ground the way the Swiss cartographer Eduard Imhof painted mountains, with light from the north-west, sunlit slopes warm and shaded slopes cool and bluish.

12 Changing a border

On the map you can click a country and remove a border or add one, and the map is drawn again. A build from scratch would rearrange the whole world, so an edited map starts from the old one. Countries start where they were, and a weak spring pulls each one back toward its old spot while the discs settle. The exclave planned in step 2 stays, and the repairs work out the others again. An added border becomes a border if the map can still be drawn flat with it, and an exclave if not.

The cells’ points are nudged by a value computed from where they are, never by a running sequence of random numbers, so land the edit doesn’t reach gets the same cells as before. Every random choice in the shaking is tied to places on the map in the same way. That keeps most of the map, though not all of it. An edit still reshapes many countries beyond the ones edited, and the project lists that among its known flaws.
